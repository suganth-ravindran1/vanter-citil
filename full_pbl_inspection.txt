P104 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text: 
P105 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Traditional network intrusion detection systems classify traffic only upon session completion, delaying response to fast-onset attacks such as Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) and brute-force attempts, while binary alert outputs contribute significantly to analyst alert fatigue in Security Operations Centre (SOC) environments. We propose a flow-level IDS using a Random Forest classifier trained on the CICIDS2017 dataset — comprising 3,119,345 labeled network flows across 15 attack classes — augmented with two novel contributions: time-windowed early detection that evaluates classification performance at 1-second and 5-second partial-flow windows, and SHAP-based explainable risk scoring that converts model confidence scores into actionable HIGH, MEDIUM, and LOW alert tiers with per-prediction feature attribution. Our model achieves 99.31% overall accuracy; volume-based attacks (DDoS, PortScan) are reliably detected within 1 second of flow onset (F1=0.999), while HIGH-tier risk alerts achieve 99.42% precision, reducing analyst review burden to 20.3% of total network traffic. These results demonstrate that combining early detection with explainability directly addresses two documented SOC operational pain points without requiring deep packet inspection or live traffic capture.

P139 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: Background
P140 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: The rapid growth of internet-connected systems has made network security one of the most critical concerns for organisations of all sizes. Cyberattacks including Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) floods, port scanning, brute-force credential attacks, botnet command traffic, and web application exploits cause billions of dollars in damages annually and disrupt services that millions of users depend on. The increasing volume and sophistication of these attacks make manual monitoring infeasible, driving demand for automated Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) that can identify malicious traffic without human intervention[1]. Traditional IDS solutions fall into two categories: signature-based systems, which match traffic against known attack patterns, and anomaly-based systems, which flag deviations from a baseline of normal behaviour. Signature-based systems are effective against known attacks but fail against novel or zero-day threats. Anomaly-based systems are more general but suffer from high false positive rates. The widespread adoption of encrypted traffic protocols (HTTPS, TLS, VPN) has further limited payload-inspection approaches, as encrypted content cannot be directly analysed. This shift has pushed the field toward flow-level feature analysis — examining the statistical properties of network conversations (packet timing, size, rate, flag counts) rather than their content, which remains effective even on encrypted traffic. Machine learning-based IDS trained on flow-level features have shown strong performance on benchmark datasets. However, most existing work stops at reporting high accuracy on completed network flows — a limitation that does not reflect real SOC operational requirements. Security analysts need to know: (1) how quickly can an attack be flagged, not just whether it can be flagged after the fact, and (2) why is the system raising this alert, not just that it is. This project directly targets both gaps.

P142 | Align: None | Style: List Paragraph | Text: “Can a machine learning model trained on flow-level network statistics reliably detect different attack types before a network session completes, and can its predictions be made explainable and actionable enough to reduce alert fatigue in a Security Operations Centre environment?”
P143 | Align: None | Style: List Paragraph | Text:

P152 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: 1.4 Scope and Limitations
P153 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: This project operates entirely on the CICIDS2017 benchmark dataset — pre-recorded, labeled network flow statistics extracted using CICFlowMeter. The scope includes multi-class attack classification, time-windowed early detection simulation, SHAP explainability, and risk scored alert generation. The system is implemented as an offline prototype with a Flask web interface for demonstration purposes. The following are explicitly out of scope: live network packet capture or real-time traffic processing; deep packet inspection or payload-level analysis; adversarial robustness testing; deployment on a production network; and evaluation on datasets other than CICIDS2017. The truncated-flow experiment approximates partial flow observation by scaling cumulative features proportionally — this is a simulation, not true sub-flow re-extraction from raw PCAPs. These limitations are documented in detail in Section 6.4.

P157 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text: 2.1.1 Classical Machine Learning for IDS 
P158 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Sharafaldin et al. [1] introduced the CICIDS2017 dataset and demonstrated baseline classification using Random Forest and other classical ML algorithms, reporting accuracy above 97% on individual attack categories. Their work established the dataset as the de facto benchmark for flow-level IDS research, but evaluated only on completed flows without considering detection speed. Lashkari et al. [2] used the same dataset with a focus on feature selection, demonstrating that a subset of 20 features retained near-full classification performance, reducing computational overhead — a finding relevant to real-time deployment but not explored in the early-detection context.
P159 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text:  2.1.2 Deep Learning Approaches 
P160 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Vinayakumar et al. [3] applied deep neural networks including LSTM and CNN architectures to network intrusion detection, arguing that temporal dependencies in packet sequences provide richer signal than static flow statistics. While achieving strong accuracy, these approaches require substantially more computational resources and training data than classical ML, making them less practical for resource-constrained deployments. Additionally, their black-box nature makes explainability — a key requirement for SOC integration — difficult to achieve without additional post-hoc methods.
P161 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text:  2.1.3 Early and Incremental Detection
P162 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text:  Early detection in network security has been studied in the context of DDoS mitigation, where response time is critical. Ring et al. [4] surveyed flow-based anomaly detection methods and noted that most academic benchmarks assume full-flow feature availability, creating a gap between reported performance and deployable performance. Work on early traffic classification — primarily in the Quality of Service domain — has shown that the first few packets of a flow carry disproportionate discriminating information for common traffic types, supporting our hypothesis that fast-onset attacks may be detectable early.
P163 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text:  2.1.4 Explainable AI in Cybersecurity
P164 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text:  Lundberg and Lee [5] introduced SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), providing a unified framework for interpreting any ML model prediction in terms of feature contributions. Subsequent work applying SHAP to network security (including IDS) has shown that flow level features such as packet rate, flow duration, and flag counts are consistently the most influential predictors across attack types, aligning with domain knowledge about how different attacks manifest in network behaviour. 
P165 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text: 2.1.5 Alert Fatigue in SOC Environments 
P166 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Veerasamy et al. [6] documented that SOC analysts in enterprise environments face thousands of undifferentiated alerts daily, with false positive rates in commercial IDS products frequently exceeding 40%. The resulting analyst fatigue leads to genuine threats being overlooked due to volume overload. Risk-ranked alert prioritisation — assigning severity scores to alerts based on confidence and context — has been proposed as a mitigation, but implementations in academic IDS prototypes remain sparse.

P212 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: 4.4 Final Approach
P213 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: The final system combines the Iteration 2 classifier with two additional components: SHAP-based feature attribution and confidence-based risk scoring. The Random Forest algorithm was selected as the final model based on three criteria specific to this task: (1) it provides native feature importance scores that align well with SHAP TreeExplainer, which computes exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time; (2) it handles high-dimensional tabular data (70 features) without requiring feature scaling; and (3) it is robust to the remaining class imbalance via the class_weight parameter.

P295 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: 6.1 Evaluation Metrics
P296 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Overall accuracy (correct predictions / total predictions) is reported for context but is not the primary metric, as the severe class imbalance in CICIDS2017 (72.8% BENIGN) means a model predicting BENIGN for every sample would achieve 72.8% accuracy trivially. The primary metrics are per-class precision, recall, and F1-score. F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, penalising both false positives and false negatives equally, making it appropriate for imbalanced multi-class classification. For the risk scoring evaluation, we report precision of HIGH-tier alerts specifically on non-BENIGN predictions, as this directly measures the operational utility of the alert prioritisation: what fraction of HIGH alerts that an analyst would review are genuine attacks.

P306 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: 6.3 Discussion
P307 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: The early detection results reveal a clear separation between two categories of attack. Volume-based attacks — DDoS, PortScan, DoS variants — are characterised by abnormally high packet rates and/or large numbers of short connections, making their statistical signature immediately visible even at a 1-second time window. DDoS flows, for instance, exhibit Flow Packets/s values orders of magnitude above normal traffic within the first second, making the F1=0.999 result at the 1-second window unsurprising in retrospect. This finding has direct operational value: SOC playbooks for DDoS response can be triggered within 1 second of flow onset rather than waiting for session termination.
P308 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: Behaviour-based attacks — Infiltration, Web Attack variants — tell a different story. Infiltration achieves F1=0.000 at 1 second: its early statistical profile is indistinguishable from normal BENIGN traffic, only revealing its nature through the full sequence of requests. The jump from 0.400 at 5 seconds to 0.857 at full flow confirms that the discriminating signal accumulates over the course of the session. This has a different operational implication: the system should not be expected to alert on Infiltration early, and a tiered detection strategy (fast-flagging volume attacks, patient monitoring of slow attacks) is more appropriate than a single latency target.
P309 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: The risk scoring results are the most directly actionable. A precision of 99.42% on HIGH-tier alerts means that when the system rates an alert as HIGH, it is correct 994 times out of every 1000 — compared to the overall 20.34% attack rate in the flagged flow set. This is the core alert-fatigue reduction mechanism: by reviewing only HIGH-confidence predictions, an analyst sees a queue that is 99.42% genuine attacks rather than a raw alert stream where 80% of alerts might be false positives. The analyst review burden of 19.7% (HIGH attack alerts / total flows) may seem high but reflects that 20.3% of test traffic was actually attacks — the system is not generating spurious alerts for BENIGN traffic, it is flagging genuine attack flows with high confidence.

P311 | Align: None | Style: Heading 2 | Text: 6.4 Limitations
P312 | Align: None | Style: Normal | Text: 
P313 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text:   Several limitations of this study warrant candid acknowledgement. First, all evaluation is conducted on a static pre-recorded dataset (CICIDS2017, July 2017); the model has not been tested on traffic from different networks, time periods, or equipment configurations. Real-world network traffic distributions vary significantly from benchmark datasets. Second, the truncated-flow experiment is a simulation: cumulative features are scaled proportionally by time ratio, which approximates but does not precisely replicate true sub-flow statistics that would be obtained by re-processing raw PCAPs through CICFlowMeter at each time point. Third, minority classes (SQL Injection: 17 test samples, Heartbleed: 2 test samples, Infiltration: 7 test samples) have sample sizes too small for statistically reliable F1 estimates. Fourth, SMOTE-based oversampling was not applied due to memory constraints; class_weight="balanced" partially compensates but cannot generate novel minority-class patterns. Fifth, the SHAP analysis was computed on a 500-flow sample rather than the full test set due to computational cost; larger samples might shift feature rankings marginally.

P367 | Align: JUSTIFY (3) | Style: Normal | Text: This project designed, implemented, and evaluated a flow-level Network Intrusion Detection System combining Random Forest classification, time-windowed early detection, and SHAP-based explainable risk scoring on the CICIDS2017 dataset. The early detection experiment demonstrates that volume-based attacks (DDoS, PortScan, DoS variants) are reliably detectable within 1 second of flow onset (F1≥0.994), while behaviour-based attacks (Infiltration, Web Attacks) require full session observation — a finding with direct implications for SOC response time planning. The driving question — whether ML on flow-level features can enable both early detection and actionable, explainable alerts — is answered affirmatively for the majority of attack classes in the CICIDS2017 benchmark.
